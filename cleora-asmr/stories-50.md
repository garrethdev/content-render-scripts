Source references are internal carousel IDs. This is a creative adaptation batch for review, without a grading gate or independent claim-check pass.

## MC-006 — The organ that was already there

**0–4 seconds**

A **new organ**. Already inside you. In 2017, the mesentery made headlines after researchers described it as one continuous structure. Anatomists had seen the tissue for centuries. They had been looking at it differently.

**4–8 seconds**

What looked like separate folds was a connected structure supporting your intestines, carrying blood vessels, nerves and lymphatics. The discovery did not add something to the body. It changed how we understood what was there.

Source: p134, slides 2–6, 10

## MC-007 — The woman writing medicine in the 1100s

**0–4 seconds**

She wrote about digestion. Sleep. Food. Nearly **900 years** ago. Hildegard of Bingen was a German abbess writing about the body while women rarely held that kind of authority in medicine.

**4–8 seconds**

Her books catalogued plants and explored illness, emotion and daily habits. She also corresponded with powerful church leaders and rulers. Centuries before wellness had an industry, this woman had already filled books with her questions.

Source: p136, slides 2–9

## MC-010 — The herbalist who challenged nineteenth-century medicine

**Bloodletting. Mercury.** That was part of medicine in the early 1800s. Samuel Thomson, a self-taught American herbalist, offered something different. People distrustful of conventional treatment started following his system instead.

**4–8 seconds**

His approach used herbs and steam baths, built around his belief that warmth restored health. Thomsonian medicine spread widely. Its rise tells a striking story about what happens when patients stop trusting the treatment they are offered.

## MC-012 — The bottle had not changed. The microscope had.

**0–4 seconds**

Clear water. **Invisible plastic.** In 2024, researchers used a new imaging method to look deeper into bottled water. They found particles previous tests could miss. The average count was roughly 240,000 per litre in the samples studied.

**4–8 seconds**

Most were nanoplastics, smaller than the particles people usually picture. The striking part was the timing: the water had not suddenly transformed overnight. Scientists had developed a way to see much more of what was already there.

Source: p095, slides 4–6

**4–8 seconds**

Could some of that light carry information between cells? Researchers are investigating the possibility. The emissions are measurable; the communication idea is still a question. Even without the answer, the body turns out to be less dark than it looks.

## MC-016 — The missing mineral

**0–4 seconds**

He was 46. Walking became difficult. His hands and feet went numb. Doctors considered neurological disorders. Then a nutritional test revealed a different clue. **Copper deficiency.** Severe enough to affect his nerves and blood cells.

**4–8 seconds**

His zinc was elevated too. Excess zinc can interfere with copper absorption. After copper treatment, his blood counts and some symptoms improved, but not all the nerve damage resolved. The missing mineral was only half the story.

## MC-018 — The recycling crew inside your cells

**0–4 seconds**

**Damaged mitochondria.** Your cells can dismantle them. Scientist Anurag Singh has spent years studying this recycling process and what happens as it falters with age. Energy production has a maintenance problem hiding behind it.

**4–8 seconds**

The process is called mitophagy: cells selectively remove worn-out mitochondria while renewing their machinery. The body does more than make energy. It also has to look after the tiny structures doing the work.

Source: p057, slides 2, 5–7

## MC-019 — The ovaries had not gone quiet

**4–8 seconds**

## MC-020 — The fast that still included food

**0–4 seconds**

Five days. **Still eating.** Researcher Valter Longo wanted to study fasting-like effects without a water-only fast. His team developed a low-calorie, plant-based program, then investigated what repeated monthly cycles changed inside the body.

**4–8 seconds**

In a small MRI subgroup, researchers observed reductions in liver and visceral fat after three cycles. The interesting twist was the design: participants were eating throughout an experiment built to imitate aspects of going without food.

## MC-021 — The surgeon who studied electricity

**0–4 seconds**

Salamanders **regrow limbs**. Humans do not. Surgeon Robert O. Becker wanted to understand the difference. His research led him toward something you cannot see at a wound: the electrical signals produced by living tissue.

**4–8 seconds**

He investigated how those signals relate to healing and regeneration, then explored the ideas in his 1985 book, The Body Electric. A missing limb raised a bigger question: how does a body know what to rebuild?

Source: Holisticentral batch 2 · p110

## MC-022 — The cancer clue that became an image

**0–4 seconds**

**Oxygen available.** Cells behaving strangely. Otto Warburg studied how cancer cells processed glucose even when oxygen was present. The unusual pattern became known as the Warburg effect. Decades later, it would help doctors look inside the body.

**4–8 seconds**

Many cancer cells take up more glucose than surrounding tissue. Some PET scans use a radioactive glucose tracer to make that activity visible. A question about how cells fuel themselves became part of how doctors find disease.

Source: Holisticentral batch 2 · p132

**4–8 seconds**

**4–8 seconds**
